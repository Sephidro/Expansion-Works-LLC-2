Consulting Inquiry Follow-Up Emails: What to Send and When

Write like you're trying to help them, not sell them. And answer fast enough that they're still in the mindset that made them reach out.

By Xavier Pearson · Updated August 15, 2026 · 5 min read

Most follow-up emails read like a form, because they were written to sound "professional," and professional usually just means generic. The alternative isn't a trick or a template. It's writing the email the way you'd actually explain something to a person standing in front of you, if your goal was to help them decide, not to close them.

Advice, Not A Pitch

If someone asks "does this work for my situation," don't answer with a feature list. Answer the actual question, including the honest edge case where it might not be a fit. People can tell the difference between someone answering them and someone reciting a script. The first one is the one they trust enough to reply to.

Ask, Don't Tell

A question invites a response. A statement invites silence. "Here's why we're a great fit" ends the conversation before it starts, because there's nothing in it to reply to. "What's actually happening right now when a lead goes quiet on you?" gives them something to answer, and the answer tells you exactly what to say next instead of you guessing at it.

This was never that complicated. It's just talking to someone the way you would in person, instead of the way you think a business is supposed to sound.

Why Speed Still Wins Even With The Best Copy In The World

None of this matters if it arrives two days later. The mechanism the speed-to-lead guide covers, that the first real response usually wins the conversation, works because the person is still in the headspace that made them reach out in the first place. Answer while they're still thinking about it, and a genuinely helpful, human-sounding response lands. Answer three days later and even a great email is competing with a decision they've already half-made without you.

Where AI Actually Helps, And Where It Doesn't

I use AI to draft faster and to catch the generic version I would've sent if I were rushing, not to replace the judgment about what to actually say. The failure mode was never "a machine wrote it." It's copy that could've been sent to anyone, whether a person or a model wrote it. The test is the same either way: would this specific paragraph make sense sent to literally any lead, or only to this one? If it's the first, rewrite it before it goes out, no matter who or what drafted it.
